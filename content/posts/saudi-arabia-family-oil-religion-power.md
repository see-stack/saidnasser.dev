## Question

How does a single family maintain control of a territory the size of Western Europe, through oil shocks, religious upheaval, and a breakneck modernization program?

## Context

Saudi Arabia is often described as an absolute monarchy, but that label misses the complexity. It is more accurately understood as a tripartite compact between:

- The House of Saud (the royal family, ~15,000 members)
- The religious establishment (descendants of the Wahhabi movement)
- The technocratic class (increasingly powerful under MBS)

Each of these groups has its own power base, economic interests, and ideological commitments. The stability of the state depends on the balance between them.

## Map

The system has four primary forces:

### 1. The Family (Al Saud)

The royal family operates as a corporate entity. Key positions (governors, ministers, military commands) are distributed across branches of the family. Internal consensus is maintained through a combination of patronage, marriage alliances, and --- when necessary --- internal purges. The 2017 Ritz-Carlton arrests were not a sign of instability; they were a maintenance operation.

### 2. Oil

Oil provides roughly 70% of state revenue and 90% of export earnings. This gives the state enormous autonomy --- it does not need to tax its citizens, which means it does not need their consent in the way a tax-dependent state does. The rentier state model creates a specific kind of politics: citizens become stakeholders in the state's continued existence rather than claimants against it.

### 3. Religion

The alliance between the House of Saud and the Wahhabi movement dates to 1744. It gives the state religious legitimacy and gives the religious establishment political protection and funding. This compact has been strained by modernization: women driving, entertainment opening, tourism expanding. But the religious establishment has adapted --- partly because its leadership is appointed by the king.

### 4. Modernization (Vision 2030)

MBS's reform program is an attempt to diversify away from oil and create a post-oil economy. It involves massive infrastructure projects (NEOM, the Red Sea project), social liberalization, and an aggressive foreign investment push. The tension is obvious: Vision 2030 requires opening the country in ways that threaten the religious compact and the patronage system that keeps the family united.

## Interpretation

Saudi Arabia is best understood as a machine for converting geological wealth into political stability. The oil revenue flows through the state, which distributes it through patronage, welfare, and infrastructure. The religious establishment provides legitimacy. The family manages the whole apparatus.

The risk is that modernization succeeds too slowly (running out of oil before the economy diversifies) or too quickly (destabilizing the religious and family compacts before new sources of legitimacy are established).

## Modern Connection

Every state built on resource extraction faces a version of this problem. Nigeria, Russia, Venezuela, and Norway all have variants of the resource curse --- but each plays out differently depending on the institutional context. Saudi Arabia's version is distinctive because the family, the religion, and the resource are so tightly integrated.

## Open Questions

- Can a rentier state successfully transition to a productive economy without democratizing?
- What happens to the religious compact when AI and entertainment make global culture instantly accessible to every Saudi with a smartphone?
- Will NEOM and similar projects create genuine economic diversification, or are they primarily vehicles for capital recycling?
